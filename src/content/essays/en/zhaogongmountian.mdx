His thoughts drift back to March 1, 2025. Whenever he wanted to put pen to paper and record it, trivial matters would get in the way, and inspiration would vanish in an instant like fireworks. Yet leaving it unwritten felt regrettable. So this guy decided to write something after all.

This was the first time this guy had climbed a mountain alone. He wanted to welcome that special day in this special way. Other hikers traveled in groups, and at times he felt lonely, but then he thought: wasn’t this also a kind of freedom? Walking alone heightened his every sense, and the air of freedom grew ever stronger. Every photograph froze a story that belonged to him—sometimes a moment of wonder at nature’s tranquility, sometimes a complaint about the treacherous mountain path.

This was the first time he had challenged a mountain this dangerous. As the elevation rose, the weather grew steadily worse. Seeing other hikers with “ice cleats” that looked like iron chains strapped to their boots, he only belatedly realized that they were miraculous anti-slip devices. At that moment, however, he had only one trekking pole and one glove, with no idea how perilous the road ahead would be. The trail was steep and the snow slick; on some stretches, the only way forward was to cling tightly to ropes and climb. A cliff lay right beside him, and the consequences of losing his grip and slipping were unthinkable. Fortunately, he advanced one careful step at a time and finally let out a long breath when he saw the iconic “Apple Tree.” Other hikers were astonished that he had brought no ice cleats. All he could do was give a wry smile and say that he hadn’t known there would be snow on the mountain—when in truth, he hadn’t even known what ice cleats were before this.

Looking down the bottomless slope beneath the ropes, he, too, had thought about giving up. In the end, though, he held on. At the summit, he took off his backpack and, with it, laid down his taut nerves. He said nothing, yet at that moment he seemed to have found the meaning of perseverance.

While resting, he heard that a newly opened route down the mountain was easier to walk. This guy valued his life and decided to play it safe, so he followed the vague directions and started on his way back. At first, he thought the descent might be easier, only to find that there was not another soul on the trail. The lively summit receded behind him, and all around there was nothing but a vast expanse of white snow. Perhaps, he thought, very few people knew about the new route. At that moment, the snowy wilderness seemed to belong to him alone. He was free.

Yet the farther he went, the steeper the trail became. Without ice cleats, he could only inch forward with extreme caution. There was no one ahead, and a flicker of panic rose inside him: had he taken the wrong path? But turning back was no longer possible; he could only steel himself and keep going down. At last, he once again saw the familiar ropes, and his anxious heart settled a little—with ropes there, the route had to be right. Without them, he probably would already have slid into the abyss on that steep, snowy slope.

He had no idea how long he had been walking, but the foot of the mountain remained impossibly far away. He kept encouraging himself, taking one solid step after another. At last, he met the first group of people climbing up. Fully equipped, they asked in surprise, “Is the trail above easy?” This guy gave a wry smile. “No. It’s very dangerous.” When they learned that he had inched his way down step by step, they all marveled at his nerve. This guy knew perfectly well that he had merely been lucky to survive; escaping without an accident had been nothing but sheer luck.

If nothing unexpected happened, then this was exactly when something unexpected was bound to happen. The snow was too deep; this guy’s foot slipped on his very next step, and he suddenly began a “speed descent.” After his mind went completely blank, adrenaline surged through him. He drove his trekking pole into the snow with all his strength and only just managed to stop. Some hikers thought he was “skiing” for fun and joked, “This stretch is a little dangerous. Wait until you get farther down before you start descending like that.” Inside, this guy roared, “I fucking almost died just now, okay?! Skiing, my damn ass!” His heart thundered, his glove was soaked through, and the back of his hand and his foot were scraped, but he had no choice. He could only keep going.

At that moment, he understood on a profound level what it meant to say, “Going up the mountain is easy; coming down is hard.” At last, he made it out of the dangerous stretch and stepped onto a stone-paved path, where travelers gradually became more numerous. This guy let out a long breath, as though reborn. He did not marvel at his own bravery; he only felt how small and fragile human beings were before nature. He was grateful for nature’s tolerance, which had allowed him to return safely.

In the end, this guy wanted only to go home, take a hot shower, eat his fill, and then sleep long and deeply. In this way, he would welcome a new year of his life.
